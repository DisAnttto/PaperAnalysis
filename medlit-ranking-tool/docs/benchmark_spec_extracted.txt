Benchmarking Correlated-Evidence Retrieval for FDA-Facing Regulatory Justification
Executive Summary
Regulatory justification frequently depends on linking a seed record (an FDA artifact, label, trial, or paper) to other public evidence records that explain why key claims, thresholds, and performance numbers are credible in light of prior precedent. FDA’s own public guidance makes these linkage patterns explicit for devices: a 510(k) Summary must identify the predicate device, describe intended use, summarize technological characteristics compared to the predicate, and briefly discuss nonclinical and clinical tests “submitted, referenced, or relied on” to support substantial equivalence. [1] This is exactly the retrieval behavior your test should enforce: rank‑1 self‑match, then strongest “submission-useful” supporting records (predicate, FDA review package, label, trial registration, pivotal studies), then progressively weaker support and hard negatives.
For De Novo devices, FDA states that after a grant, it posts the granting order and “voluntarily generates and publicly discloses a decision summary,” intended as an “objective and balanced” summary of the scientific evidence and a resource for future substantial equivalence work. [2] For PMA devices, FDA requires an FDA-written Summary of Safety and Effectiveness Data (SSED) that summarizes device description, preclinical and clinical evidence, and FDA’s analysis—making PMA approval packages among the most correlated and highest-authority “evidence records” for many device seeds. [3]
This report designs a benchmark and deterministic implementation plan to test a retrieval system that, given any FDA-related seed, returns (a) the seed as rank‑1, then (b) the best correlated evidence records that would justify the seed’s regulatory claims. It defines a tiered gold-list schema, correlation features and scoring rules (with formulas and pseudocode), an evidence-graph model and retrieval pipeline (with mermaid diagrams), practical parsing/linking methods, evaluation metrics, and a Cursor/Opus-ready implementation prompt with concrete API endpoints (openFDA, AccessGUDID, DailyMed, ClinicalTrials.gov, NCBI E‑utilities). Key public sources include: FDA 510(k)/PMA/SSED/De Novo materials, Devices@FDA and openFDA datasets, NLM sources (AccessGUDID, DailyMed, PubMed/PMC, ClinicalTrials.gov), and postmarket adverse event/recall data (MAUDE/FAERS/recalls) for risk context. [4]
Assumptions and Scope
This design assumes the device class, device type, and therapeutic area are unspecified; the benchmark must therefore cover a mix of devices and drugs and remain robust to heterogeneous schemas and evidence patterns. The test corpus is limited to public sources only, and the system should not assume access to proprietary submission modules or internal sponsor documents. The benchmark defines “correlated” as useful for regulatory explanation, not merely semantically similar (e.g., same disease). This scope matches FDA’s emphasis that substantial equivalence and comparisons are established through intended use, technological characteristics, performance, safety, effectiveness, labeling, and related evidence—not simply topical similarity. [5]
The retrieval requirement “rank‑1 self-match” is treated as a positive control and must be preserved. The benchmark’s real discrimination begins at ranks 2–10: whether the engine surfaces predicate-linked and review/label/trial-linked records that explain the seed’s numbers/specs/claims. For drugs, this benchmark treats labeling and FDA review packages as primary regulatory artifacts and recognizes that “current” labeling (DailyMed/FDALabel) can differ from last FDA‑approved labeling (Drugs@FDA) due to permissible in-review changes, so the evidence graph must store multiple label versions with provenance. [6]
Benchmark Design
A correct benchmark for correlated-evidence retrieval must encode both what should be retrieved and why it matters for regulatory justification. FDA’s own “Content of a 510(k)” page provides a practical template for benchmark fields: 510(k) documentation must include an Indications for Use statement that specifies indications, clinical settings, population, and anatomical sites, and FDA recommends side-by-side comparisons across intended use, indications, target population, anatomical site, use environment, energy delivered, design, performance, standards met, materials, biocompatibility, sterility, and multiple safety domains. [1] These categories should be directly reflected in the benchmark’s normalized target profile and deterministic correlation features.
Tier structure and record requirements
Each benchmark target uses a tiered gold list:
Tier 0 (self_match): the seed record itself; rank‑1 expected.
Tier 1 (must-retrieve): direct product identity links and direct FDA linkages:
direct predicate (devices) or direct same-product FDA package (drugs/devices),
FDA review package / SSED / De Novo decision summary,
label (DailyMed, Drugs@FDA label PDF),
linked trial registry record (ClinicalTrials.gov),
explicit citation targets if the seed cites another study or standard.
Tier 2 (strong correlated evidence): high-value precedent and explanatory records (same ingredient/device family, same intended use and endpoint/threshold logic, same material/tech, same comparator design).
Tier 3 (acceptable support): useful context (reviews, related studies in same indication with same endpoint, but less direct).
Tier 4 (distractors): topically similar but weak for regulatory explanation.
Tier 5 (hard negatives): superficially similar by keywords but not explainable/precedent-relevant (wrong route, wrong indication, wrong technology, wrong regulatory bucket).
This tier structure is operationally motivated by FDA requirements to identify predicates and to explain differences via supporting evidence, including literature and clinical trials, especially when technological differences raise safety/effectiveness questions. [5]
Benchmark target set design
Instead of “random” targets, select 20–40 publicly reconstructable evidence chains. A practical target mix is:
Devices: 8–15 510(k), 4–8 De Novo, 4–8 PMA/SSED.
Drugs: 8–12 NDA/BLA with accessible approval packages and labels.
The selection heuristic is to prefer targets whose public artifacts explicitly mention: (a) intended use/indication, (b) technology/material/formulation, (c) quantitative endpoints or thresholds, and (d) linked trials or predicate relationships. De Novo is particularly valuable because FDA explicitly publishes decision summaries intended to summarize evidence and become a resource for future substantial equivalence work. [7] PMA targets are valuable because FDA’s SSED summarizes core evidence and analysis. [3]

Deterministic Correlation Features and Scoring
This benchmark is only useful if the retrieval system’s scoring is deterministic, auditable, and aligned with regulatory reasoning. FDA’s 510(k) Summary requirements and comparison guidance supply concrete feature categories: intended use, indications, technological characteristics (design/material/composition/energy source), standards met, performance testing (bench/animal/clinical), and explicit identification of predicate and supporting studies “submitted, referenced, or relied on.” [5] These should become deterministic pairwise similarity features between (seed, candidate).
Pairwise feature set
Define features  computed from normalized extracted fields:
Exact identifier match: PMID/PMCID/NCT/K‑number/PMA/De Novo number/BLA/NDA/SETID/UDI-DI exact equality.
Same-product identity: normalized product name + manufacturer/applicant + route/technology match.
Regulatory bucket match (devices): product code, class, regulation number (e.g., 870.2920), panel match. [8]
Intended use / indication match: overlap or entailment alignment between normalized indication sets. FDA emphasizes intended use and requires indication statements to specify population/anatomical site and be consistent with labeling. [5]
Technology/material match (devices): similarity over design/material/energy source; FDA explicitly calls these “technological characteristics” in 510(k) summaries. [1]
Route/delivery match (drugs/devices): intravitreal vs oral vs implanted; surgical placement vs external device.
Endpoint overlap: Jaccard/cosine similarity over normalized endpoints/metrics (BCVA, IOP, OCT thickness, sensitivity/specificity).
Numeric-threshold proximity: if both contain comparable numeric benchmarks (e.g., noninferiority margin, sensitivity threshold, allowable particle limits), score proximity within tolerances.
Trial-design match: RCT vs prospective cohort; comparator type and follow-up window.
Predicate/citation link: explicit edges such as “predicate device” in 510(k) summary or explicit citations in an FDA review/label. FDA’s 510(k) content guidance explicitly anticipates literature and trials as supporting evidence. [1]
Source authority: FDA review documents and SSED/De Novo summaries have higher authority than general literature; DailyMed/Drugs@FDA labels have high authority for indication and warnings; trial registry records are official structured definitions of endpoints/design. [9]
Three-score model: correlation, strength, explanation value
Your benchmark requires three scores per candidate.
Correlation score
Compute correlation as a weighted sum of deterministic similarity features, with hierarchical overrides for Tier0/Tier1.

Example weight template (normalize to 1.0 after excluding missing features):
exact linkage / explicit edge: 0.25
intended use / indication: 0.20
regulatory bucket (product code/class/regulation): 0.15
tech/material/route: 0.15
endpoint overlap: 0.10
numeric threshold proximity: 0.10
trial-design match: 0.05
This mirrors FDA’s emphasis on intended use, technological characteristics, and performance evidence. [5]
Evidence strength score
Assign evidence_strength primarily by source type (authority) with deterministic modifiers.
A practical mapping (example):
FDA SSED / De Novo decision summary / FDA Summary Review or clinical review: 1.00 [10]
FDA-approved labeling PDF (Drugs@FDA) or DailyMed SPL (in-use labeling): 0.90 [6]
ClinicalTrials.gov record (structured trial design/endpoints): 0.80 [11]
Peer-reviewed pivotal RCT paper / PMC full text: 0.75
Observational studies: 0.50
Background narrative reviews: 0.35
Postmarket surveillance (MAUDE/FAERS) as evidence of safety signals, not efficacy: 0.40, with explicit “not causal” caution [12]
Device recalls database entry (risk context): 0.50 [13]
Then apply deterministic modifiers:
+0.05 if the record is an FDA/NIH primary source (FDA/NLM/NIH domains).
−0.10 if the record is a secondary blog/press release (still usable as context, rarely Tier1).
Explanation value score
Explanation value should reward candidates that help justify the specific seed claims (numbers/specs/thresholds), not just identity.

Where claim_coverage(s,c) is the fraction of seed’s key claims that the candidate supports (endpoints, thresholds, warnings, comparator logic), and  might be 0.6 for “numbers-heavy” seeds.
Tier assignment and must-retrieve thresholds
Tier labels are deterministic functions of relation labels + scores:
Tier0: self_match always.
Tier1 (must):
relation_label in {direct_predicate, direct_same_product, direct_review, linked_trial, label} and corr ≥ 0.75, OR
evidence_strength ≥ 0.9 and corr ≥ 0.7 (typical for FDA review/label).
Tier2: corr ≥ 0.6 and explain ≥ 0.45, not Tier1.
Tier3: corr ≥ 0.4 and explain ≥ 0.25.
Tier4: corr ≥ 0.3 but fails explanation coverage or authority.
Tier5: corr < 0.3 OR explicit hard-negative rule triggers (e.g., route mismatch, different product code/class for devices, different active ingredient for drugs).
Evidence Graph and Retrieval Pipeline
A retrieval system that “thinks like a regulatory reviewer” should not be a single vector similarity query. It should build a canonical evidence graph linking the seed to direct regulatory artifacts, then expand outward by typed edges (predicate, same product identity, same endpoint/threshold logic), while maintaining provenance and determinism.

Data Sources, Parsing, and Validation
Priority sources and what they contribute
FDA’s device and drug ecosystems provide complementary artifacts that are ideal for correlated-evidence retrieval.
For devices, “Content of a 510(k)” provides both required elements and the comparison categories that should become normalization fields, including the explicit idea that supporting information can come from bench testing, animal studies, clinical studies (“information gathered from medical literature”), and clinical trials. [1] De Novo adds a “decision summary” intended to be objective and to support future substantial equivalence work, making it a particularly clean Tier1 target type. [2] PMA SSEDs are FDA documents summarizing preclinical and clinical evidence and FDA analysis, which are Tier1 by default for PMA products. [3]
For structured programmatic access, openFDA exposes machine-readable datasets for devices (510(k), PMA, classification, recalls, adverse events) and drugs (labels, Drugs@FDA, NDC, adverse events). openFDA requires HTTPS and offers standard rate limits (240 requests/minute; 120,000/day with an API key). [20] Devices@FDA is a useful aggregator that links to device summaries and user instructions across 510(k) and PMA databases. [21]
For identity normalization, AccessGUDID provides device lookup APIs keyed by DI/UDI and returns records in JSON/XML. [16] For drug labeling, NLM’s DailyMed provides “in use” labeling and publishes REST APIs to query SPLs and fetch SPL XML by SETID. [22] For trial structure, the modern ClinicalTrials.gov API v2 is REST-based and described by OpenAPI 3.0, with standardized enums and ISO 8601 dates—ideal for deterministic extraction of endpoints, comparators, and follow-up. [11] For literature, NLM/NCBI E‑utilities provide structured URL-based APIs (ESearch/ESummary/EFetch/ELink), including ELink support for finding related records and LinkOut URLs, enabling deterministic harvesting without relying on brittle HTML parsing. [23]
For risk context, MAUDE contains MDRs and is explicitly described as adverse event reporting data with important limitations; FAERS is the analogous drug adverse event database supporting postmarketing surveillance; FDA’s device recalls database provides recall entries and dates (with caveats about posting date vs initiation). [17] These sources should contribute risk modifiers and “explanation context,” not primary efficacy evidence.
Finally, FDA-recognized standards and guidance documents are essential for correlating device testing logic. FDA notes that declarations of conformity to recognized consensus standards may reduce supporting testing documentation; the recognized standards database is explicitly tied to safety and effectiveness determinations. [24]
Practical parsing and linking approaches
A correlation engine requires robust extraction and identifier linking across heterogeneous formats.
PDF parsing for FDA artifacts (SSEDs, De Novo decision summaries, 510(k) summaries, FDA reviews): Use deterministic PDF text extraction (e.g., pypdf/pdfminer) with table heuristics; store page-level provenance. FDA device artifacts often include structured sections (device description, nonclinical testing, clinical testing) that can be extracted with regex + section header lexicons. [25]
SPL parsing for labeling: Fetch SPL XML using DailyMed services/v2/spls/{SETID}.xml and parse HL7 SPL sections into normalized fields: indications, dosage/administration, warnings, contraindications, instructions for use. [26]
Trial parsing: Use ClinicalTrials.gov v2 endpoints with OpenAPI-described schemas; extract interventions, outcomes, masking, comparator, follow-up windows, populations; normalize dates and endpoint names. [11]
Literature parsing: Use NCBI E‑utilities to fetch PubMed/PMC metadata and linked records (ELink); when PMC full text exists, extract explicit numeric endpoints and study design deterministically with a combined rule-based + model-assisted pipeline. [23]
Identifier linking: Use a prioritized linkage chain: 1) exact identifiers (PMID/PMCID/NCT/K/PMA/DEN/NDA/BLA/SETID/DI/UDI),
2) application numbers and sponsor/manufacturer + product name,
3) normalized indication/route/technology and endpoint overlap.
Handling missing or ambiguous data
Benchmarks and implementations should treat missingness as “unknown,” not as neutral similarity.
Maintain per-field confidence and provenance (source and location).
Exclude missing features from correlation scoring and renormalize feature weights, rather than imputing neutral scores.
When multiple conflicting values exist (e.g., DailyMed “in use” label vs Drugs@FDA-approved label), represent them as separate nodes with timestamps and link to the same product identity; use deterministic preference rules per benchmark scenario. [6]
Validation plan
A practical validation program includes:
Retrospective predicate recovery tests (devices): For a sample of 510(k) summaries, verify the system retrieves the stated predicate device(s) as Tier1 and ranks them above same-indication distractors. This aligns with FDA’s requirement that the summary identify the legally marketed predicate and compare technological characteristics. [5]
Approval-package retrieval tests (PMA/De Novo): Ensure SSED/decision summaries are Tier1 for any seed about the same product; these artifacts summarize the evidence basis. [27]
Drug approval-package + labeling tests: For a seed paper about an approved drug, verify Tier1 retrieval of the relevant FDA review package (if public) and current label (DailyMed) + FDA-approved label (Drugs@FDA/accessdata label PDF), then linked trials and pivotal papers. [28]
Hard-negative avoidance tests: Verify that wrong-route or wrong-ingredient matches rank low even if disease keywords overlap.

Why this plan aligns with FDA-facing evidence linkage
The decisive implementation detail is that “correlated evidence” must reflect the same comparison and support categories FDA expects to see: intended use, technological characteristics, performance, standards, labeling, and supporting evidence including literature and trials.
